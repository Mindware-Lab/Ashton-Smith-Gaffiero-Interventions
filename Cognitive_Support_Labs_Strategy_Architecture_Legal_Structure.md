# Cognitive Support Labs

## Building a joint applied-cognition company

**Prepared for Dr Daniel Gaffiero**  
**Strategic discussion paper — July 2026**

---

# 1. The opportunity

Cognitive Support Labs (name?) should not be conceived simply as a company that produces one or two digital health apps.

The stronger opportunity is to build a **research-led intervention and service-intelligence company** that can repeatedly:

```text
→ identify where support is failing
→ decide which problems are worth solving
→ design and test interventions
→ build the necessary digital products and service tools
→ measure whether they work
→ integrate them into real institutions
→ publish, license and scale the strongest solutions
```

The initial application would be health psychology, where Dan brings the strongest domain expertise, institutional integration and publication track record.

The deeper capability platform, however, could later be applied to other institutional systems, including corporate resilience, cognitive performance, education and insurance. The company would therefore begin in health psychology without being permanently limited to one condition, one product or one sector.

The strategic principle is:

> **Build one shared capability platform, prove it through a focused health-psychology application, and then extend it into additional sectors with appropriate domain leadership.**

---

# 2. The company thesis

Cognitive Support Labs would help institutions understand and improve psychological and functional support.

Its core proposition could be:

> **We identify where support is inadequate, determine which intervention opportunities offer the greatest value, develop and test proportionate solutions, and integrate the strongest interventions into real services.**

This is broader than app development.

Depending on the problem, the first useful output might be:

- a pathway or provision map;
- a service-gap analysis;
- an intervention-opportunity portfolio;
- a measurement system;
- a redesigned support pathway;
- a low-intensity digital intervention;
- a practitioner tool;
- or a combined digital and human service.

The company should therefore begin with the problem and the institutional pathway—not with a preselected app.

# 3. The shared 'six-capability' platform

The joint company would implement the full HRP Lab Enterprise Capability Model.

The capabilities form one connected sequence.

## 3.1 Understand the system

### Service-pathway, system and risk intelligence

The company first asks:

> Where is support absent, delayed, fragmented, inequitable or poorly matched to need?

Examples include:

- inconsistent adult ADHD waiting-list support across regions;
- limited early support for students with mathematics anxiety;
- gaps between pain assessment and psychological self-management;
- occupational-health pathways that identify distress but provide little functional support.

Typical outputs would include pathway maps, provision indices, user-journey analyses, service-gap reports and candidate intervention points.

This capability can itself produce commissioned revenue before an app is built.

## 3.2 Choose the right opportunity

### Intervention discovery and prioritisation

Not every service gap should become a digital product.

Candidate interventions should be assessed against factors such as:

- importance of the unmet need;
- strength of the mechanism and evidence;
- likely functional and service value;
- suitability for digital delivery;
- user and practitioner acceptability;
- implementation and regulatory burden;
- presence of a credible institutional buyer;
- potential for measurement and scale.

The result should be an explicit decision to develop, partner, defer or reject an intervention.

## 3.3 Engineer the intervention

### Transfer-protocol engineering

The HRP Transfer Lab would turn a selected opportunity into a formal, testable intervention.

The process would include:

```text
→ mechanism model
→ competing protocol designs
→ computational and synthetic-participant testing
→ prototype
→ small human calibration
→ transfer and functional tests
→ delayed re-check
→ protocol revision
```

The distinctive question is not only whether people improve on a trained exercise.

It is:

> **Does the relevant change survive new examples, changed contexts, independent tasks, meaningful functioning and delay?**

## 3.4 Measure change defensibly

### Measurement and psychometric engineering

Each project would distinguish different kinds of evidence:

```text
≠ need
≠ engagement
≠ task learning
≠ mechanism change
≠ transfer
≠ functioning
≠ service value
≠ economic value
```

The company could develop or adapt:

- validated scales;
- cognitive-affective tasks;
- functional measures;
- provision indices;
- adaptive tests;
- service dashboards;
- confidence labels;
- delayed follow-up measures.

Measurement should be designed alongside the intervention rather than added at the end.

## 3.5 Deliver and integrate the solution

### Digital experience, integration, access and adoption

A scientifically credible intervention is not useful if it cannot be adopted.

The company would therefore design the full delivery system, including:

- user-facing apps or web tools;
- onboarding and eligibility;
- practitioner or manager tools;
- accessibility and localisation;
- escalation and safeguarding;
- service workflow;
- aggregate reporting;
- technical integration;
- training and implementation support.

The product is not only the app. It is the app plus the surrounding pathway.

## 3.6 Validate, publish and mobilise the knowledge

### Research, validation, publication and knowledge mobilisation

The company would build an evidence ladder from early feasibility through to implementation and scale.

Possible outputs include:

- pathway reports;
- preregistrations;
- protocol and psychometric papers;
- feasibility and controlled studies;
- implementation studies;
- partner reports;
- health-economic analyses;
- policy and commissioning briefs;
- case studies.

Publication would strengthen the science, institutional credibility and commercial value of the platform.

## 3.7 Governance across all six

Governance, ethics, assurance and IP stewardship would run across the entire system.

This includes:

- research ethics;
- privacy and health-data rights;
- clinical and product safety;
- safeguarding;
- responsible AI;
- accessibility and equity;
- claims boundaries;
- regulatory classification;
- auditability and human override;
- background and foreground IP;
- publication and confidentiality rules.

The most important architectural distinction is between:

- Mark’s pre-existing assets;
- the new joint operating capability;

## 5.2 The joint operating company

Mark and Dan would jointly build the serious institutional business.

This could initially be Cognitive Support Labs Ltd (or some other name!), provided both agree that its future may extend beyond health psychology.

The joint company would own or control:

- new health-specific protocols;
- jointly developed pathway methods;
- intervention-specific software;
- new measures and item banks;
- synthetic participant models developed jointly;
- practitioner and service tools;
- new datasets;
- implementation methods;
- partner contracts and licences.

The company would operate the six-capability system rather than merely licensing finished products from HRP Lab.

## 5.3 Cognitive Support Labs as the first vertical

Health psychology would be the first sector application because it offers:

- Dan’s specialist expertise;
- active academic and institutional connections;
- credible research and publication routes;
- identifiable service gaps;
- grant and partnership opportunities;
- meaningful functional and service outcomes.

Initial demonstrators might include:

1. exam and mathematics anxiety;
2. chronic/persistant pain;
3. adult ADHD provision and interim support.

The first priority should be one complete demonstrator, not three simultaneous apps.

## 5.4 Later corporate expansion

After the capability system has been demonstrated in health psychology, it could be applied to corporate cognition, resilience and performance.

The same underlying capabilities would translate as follows:

| Shared capability | Health application | Corporate application |
|---|---|---|
| System intelligence | Care and support pathways | Workflows and decision systems |
| Opportunity selection | Unmet psychological support | Cognitive-load or performance bottlenecks |
| Protocol engineering | Health-psychology intervention | Attention, evidence and decision support |
| Measurement | Symptoms, functioning and service outcomes | Work quality, errors, judgement and resilience |
| Integration | Practitioner and care pathways | Teams, occupational health and work systems |
| Validation | Health and functional studies | Organisational pilots and performance studies |

This later vertical would probably require an additional leader with expertise in occupational or organisational psychology, human factors, workplace research or enterprise implementation.

The corporate branch should therefore grow from the joint platform—not from IQ Mindware as a separate institutional business.

# 9. The initial business model

Cognitive Support Labs should use a **mixed research, service and product model**.

The company should not depend entirely on grants, but nor should it expect its first meaningful income to come from app subscriptions or licences.

The most realistic development sequence is:

```text
→ commissioned pathway or provision study
→ intervention-opportunity and feasibility work
→ grant-funded or partner-funded protocol development
→ custom digital intervention or service tool
→ pilot and implementation partnership
→ institutional licence, service contract or international partnership
```

This creates several connected revenue and development routes.

## 9.1 Pathway and provision intelligence

The earliest commercial offer may be a commissioned analysis rather than a completed intervention.

Examples include:

* comparing psychological and functional support across universities;
* mapping adult ADHD waiting-list provision;
* identifying gaps within persistent-pain pathways;
* assessing digital-support readiness in another country or service system.

The output could include a pathway map, provision index, service-gap analysis and prioritised intervention opportunities.

This route creates revenue while exercising the first two HRP capabilities:

```text
understand the system
→ identify the highest-value opportunity
```

## 9.2 Intervention design and feasibility

A partner may then commission or co-fund:

* evidence and mechanism synthesis;
* theory-of-change development;
* intervention prioritisation;
* measurement planning;
* prototype and pilot specification.

This stage should determine whether the appropriate response is:

```text
a digital intervention
a practitioner tool
a redesigned pathway
a measurement system
or
a combined service
```

The company should not assume that every identified gap must become an app.

## 9.3 Grant-funded research and development

Research grants can provide the evidence engine for the company.

The proposed MRC chronic-primary-pain programme is a particularly valuable route because it could fund:

* a condition-specific digital intervention;
* the HRP Transfer Lab methodology;
* pain-specific measurement and human calibration;
* pathway and implementation work;
* controlled proof of concept;
* publication and commercial-development preparation.

The grant would not fund the whole future company or its corporate branch. Its strategic value would be to establish the first credible clinical product and a reusable intervention-development capability.

Other funding routes may support different stages, including:

* smaller feasibility and calibration studies;
* impact-acceleration work;
* NHS or service-based evaluations;
* challenge-led innovation programmes;
* international research partnerships.

Grant activity should therefore be connected to explicit product, IP and implementation outcomes.

## 9.4 Custom product and service development

A university, healthcare provider, insurer, medical scheme or international partner could fund a tailored package comprising:

```text
pathway analysis
+ co-design
+ protocol and measurement development
+ software or practitioner-tool development
+ pilot evaluation
+ implementation support
```

Custom development should use reusable CSL infrastructure wherever possible while preserving the partner’s specific pathway, population and governance requirements.

## 9.5 Institutional access and licensing

Once an intervention is sufficiently validated, revenue may come through:

* institutional licences;
* service contracts;
* per-user or eligible-population access;
* implementation and reporting agreements;
* white-labelled products;
* protocol or software licensing;
* country or sector partnerships.

The preferred long-term model is not necessarily for CSL to operate every service directly.

A scalable route may be:

```text
→ central scientific and digital platform
→ local institutional or delivery partner
→ shared implementation and validation
→ licence or revenue share
```

## 9.6 Commercial principle

The business should combine:

```text
+ commissioned work
+ grant-funded evidence development
+ partner-funded product development
+ institutional licensing
```

This reduces dependence on consumer sales without making the enterprise wholly dependent on uncertain research funding.

The first sale need not be an app licence.

It may be a pathway-intelligence or intervention-design project that leads to a larger research, development and implementation partnership.

# 10. Development strategy

The operating principle should be:

# Capability-complete, product-thin

Cognitive Support Labs should not attempt to build a universal platform before it has a funded or institutionally supported problem.

The first substantial project should create a minimum working version of the complete capability chain:

```text
→ one pathway analysis
→ one opportunity-selection process
→ one intervention protocol
→ one measurement system
→ one digital and service workflow
→ one validation and publication programme
```

Reusable platform assets should then be extracted from real projects.

## Phase 1: Founder, legal and institutional foundation

Mark and Dan should first agree:

* the joint company vision/mission;
* founder roles, time commitments and decision rights;
* background and foreground IP principles;
* the relationship between HRP Lab, CSL and KCL;
* publication and confidentiality rules;
* the first target institutional problem;
* the initial funding and commercial routes.

The main outputs should be:

```text
founder heads of terms
background-IP schedule
provisional licence framework
decision-rights agreement
institutional and publication boundaries
first-project selection criteria
```

## Phase 2: Secure an anchor

Before substantial platform development, the company should secure at least one credible anchor:

* an MRC or other funded programme;
* a commissioned pathway study;
* a university pilot partner;
* a health-service or provider collaboration;
* an international co-development partner;
* a partner willing to support recruitment, implementation or adoption.

The MRC chronic-primary-pain proposal could serve as the principal evidence and capability-development anchor, provided the pain-specific readiness criteria are met.

The company should also pursue nearer-term commissioned or feasibility work rather than waiting solely for the MRC outcome.

## Phase 3: Build the first complete capability chain

The first project should demonstrate that CSL can move from institutional need to credible intervention and evidence.

Two linked routes are currently plausible.

### Clinical anchor

```text
→ chronic-primary-pain pathway
→ mechanism and product development
→ HRP Transfer Lab calibration
→ active-controlled proof of concept
→ functional and delayed outcomes
→ NHS or provider implementation route
```

### Enabling or lower-risk demonstrator

```text
→ exam and mathematics-anxiety provision
→ support-gap analysis
→ digital protocol and measures
→ university pilot
→ publication and institutional case
```

The two projects should remain scientifically distinct, but they can contribute to a shared methodology and technical infrastructure.

## Phase 4: Consolidate the shared platform

After the first projects, CSL should extract and formalise reusable assets such as:

* pathway-intelligence methods;
* intervention-prioritisation tools;
* computational and synthetic-participant workflows;
* software components;
* measurement and scoring systems;
* governance templates;
* implementation methods;
* commercial and licensing packages.

This is the point at which the HRP Transfer Lab becomes a demonstrated operating capability rather than only a proposed general platform.

## Phase 5: Add a second health application or international route

Expansion should initially remain close to the validated capability base.

Possible routes include:

* a second health-psychology indication;
* adult ADHD pathway and interim-support work;
* an additional pain or university partner;
* localisation and testing in South Africa;
* a private-provider, insurer or medical-scheme partnership.

The company should expand only where there is:

```text
a defined institutional problem
+ a credible domain partner
+ a funding or purchasing route
+ measurable implementation value
```

## Phase 6: Corporate translation

A later possible corporate vertical should follow evidence that the shared capability model works in at least one institutional health context.

CSL should then:

1. recruit an appropriate corporate-domain lead;
2. apply pathway and system intelligence to an actual organisation;
3. identify a specific cognitive or workflow bottleneck;
4. select a proportionate intervention;
5. develop corporate-specific outcomes and governance;
6. run an organisational pilot before creating a general corporate product.

The corporate branch should therefore emerge from the demonstrated platform rather than from a rebranding of the existing IQ Mindware consumer offer.

# 14. Funding and enterprise-development routes

Cognitive Support Labs should combine grants, commissioned work and commercial partnerships rather than depend on any single funding route.

The emerging development sequence is:

```text
BPS feasibility study
→ pain-specific translational development
→ proof of concept
→ implementation and licensing
```

## Current enabling study

Mark and Dan have jointly submitted a **BPS Small Research Grant proposal** for a 12-month mathematics-anxiety feasibility study hosted by King’s College London.

The study would test:

* remote delivery of a digital cognitive intervention;
* relative-frame attention-control training;
* directional transfer between absolute and relative tasks;
* an active-control condition;
* task reliability, adherence and data quality;
* independent attention and arithmetic performance under evaluative pressure;
* delayed follow-up.

Its purpose is not to prove clinical or educational efficacy. It is to establish whether the task, transfer-sensitive measures and digital delivery system are sufficiently feasible and informative to justify a larger trial.

If funded, it would provide the first empirical collaboration between Mark and Dan and an enabling calibration case for the wider Transfer Lab methodology.

## Main translational route

The proposed **MRC Proof of Concept programme in chronic primary pain** could become the principal route for developing:

* the first substantial health-psychology intervention;
* the HRP Transfer Lab;
* condition-specific measurement and computational models;
* clinical and pathway partnerships;
* controlled human proof of concept;
* functional and delayed outcomes;
* publications and foreground intellectual property.

The MRC programme should remain focused on one credible pain intervention and route to patient benefit. The reusable CSL platform would emerge from that work rather than being presented as the primary funded product.

If the pain programme is not sufficiently mature for the full MRC application, a smaller **MRC Impact Acceleration** or equivalent award could address one critical development gap before a later proof-of-concept submission.

## Commissioned and partner-funded work

Grant decisions are uncertain and slow. CSL should therefore develop a parallel institutional offer:

> **Cognitive Support Pathway and Intervention Opportunity Study**

A university, provider, charity, insurer, medical scheme or international partner could commission CSL to:

```text
map current provision
→ identify gaps and inequalities
→ prioritise intervention opportunities
→ recommend a pilot and measurement plan
```

This can generate earlier revenue and may lead to:

* intervention co-design;
* custom app or practitioner-tool development;
* pilot evaluation;
* implementation support;
* institutional licensing.

The first sale therefore need not be an app licence. It may be a pathway or feasibility project that creates the basis for a larger partnership.

## Later-stage routes

* KCL innovation and spinout support;
* a future Knowledge Transfer Partnership;
* private-provider or insurer co-development;

These routes should be matched to product maturity rather than pursued simultaneously.

# Conclusion

The strategic opportunity is not to revamp Mark's existing consumer-app business or create a small collection of health apps.

It is to use Mark’s accumulated scientific and technical assets, together with Dan’s health-psychology expertise and institutional research capability, as seeds to build a joint institutional enterprise.

The proposed structure is:

```text
HRP Lab Ltd
holds Mark’s pre-existing IP, software
and bounded IQ Mindware activity

        ↓ licences selected assets into

Cognitive Support Labs Ltd
Mark and Dan jointly build and operate
the six-capability institutional platform

        ↓ developed initially through

funded and commissioned health-psychology projects

        ↓ supported by

a wider clinical, methodological,
engineering and commercial team

        ↓ later extended into

corporate and other institutional verticals
with additional domain leadership
```

The MRC chronic-primary-pain programme could provide a particularly valuable route for developing:

* the first clinical intervention;
* the HRP Transfer Lab methodology;
* measurement and validation infrastructure;
* the research and publication network;
* clinical and implementation partnerships;
* foreground intellectual property.

It should be treated as the potential **evidence engine** for the enterprise—not as the entire business model.

In parallel, CSL should develop commissioned pathway and intervention-intelligence work, commercial partnerships and alternative funding routes.

The first objective remains one complete, repeatable capability:

```text
one important institutional problem
→ one defensible pathway analysis
→ one prioritised intervention opportunity
→ one scientifically grounded protocol
→ one usable digital or service solution
→ one credible study
→ one institutional implementation case
```

If that chain works, CSL will possess something substantially more valuable than another app:

> **A repeatable system for turning psychological and cognitive science into evidence-generating institutional interventions.**

# Executive proposition

Cognitive Support Labs has the potential to become more than a company producing one digital health app.

The stronger opportunity is to build a reusable **health-psychology intervention and service-intelligence platform** capable of:

```text
understanding where support is absent, delayed, fragmented or inequitable
→ identifying and prioritising the highest-value intervention opportunities
→ selecting promising cognitive-affective mechanisms
→ designing and stress-testing intervention protocols
→ creating customised digital applications and service tools
→ measuring mechanism, transfer, functioning and service value
→ integrating products into real institutional pathways
→ validating, publishing and progressively strengthening the interventions
→ scaling them through institutional and international partnerships
```

This model aligns Cognitive Support Labs with the full HRP Lab Enterprise Capability Model:

```text
1. Service-pathway, system and risk intelligence
2. Intervention discovery and prioritisation
3. Transfer-protocol engineering
4. Measurement and psychometric engineering
5. Digital experience, integration, access and adoption
6. Research, validation, publication and knowledge mobilisation

Across all six:
govern ethically, protect rights, manage risk and IP,
and preserve appropriate human authority.
```

Big Health provides an important model for the downstream part of this system.

It has built a business that converts evidence-based psychological interventions into attractive, accessible digital products distributed through health systems, providers, employers and payers. In the United Kingdom, its products demonstrate how digital psychological support can be publicly funded rather than requiring every user to purchase a consumer subscription. In the United States, its portfolio includes regulated prescription products alongside non-prescription programmes and is increasingly integrated with provider workflows and institutionally funded access.

The central lesson is:

> **The intervention is only one part of the product. The complete product includes evidence, digital experience, professional integration, payment, governance, implementation and measurable value.**

Cognitive Support Labs could adopt this model while adding a broader upstream capability:

> **A computationally assisted system for identifying where psychological and functional support is inadequate, determining which intervention opportunities are most valuable, and designing, customising and testing the strongest solutions before committing to expensive large-scale development.**

This system would combine:

- Dan’s health-psychology, cognitive-affective and behaviour-change expertise;
- Mark’s cognitive-neuroscience, transfer-protocol and digital-product architecture;
- the HRP Transfer Lab’s computational modelling, synthetic-participant and experimental infrastructure;
- pathway and provision intelligence across institutional systems;
- measurement and psychometric engineering;
- modular application and practitioner-tool development;
- implementation science and service integration;
- university, healthcare, private-provider, employer and international partnerships.

The long-term result could be a company that repeatedly discovers, builds, validates and scales evidence-led interventions rather than depending on the success of one or two standalone apps.

# 1. What the Big Health model teaches us

Big Health’s development can be represented as a clear strategic sequence:

```text
identify one important and underserved problem
→ digitise an established psychological intervention
→ create a strong user experience
→ build a substantial evidence base
→ demonstrate clinical and economic value
→ secure institutional access and reimbursement
→ expand into adjacent conditions
```

There are seven main lessons for Cognitive Support Labs.

## 1.1 Begin with a bounded problem

Big Health did not begin by promising to improve mental health in general.

Its flagship programme addressed insomnia:

- a widespread and functionally important problem;
- an established first-line psychological treatment;
- insufficient specialist access;
- a structured intervention suitable for digital delivery;
- clear clinical and functional outcomes.

Cognitive Support Labs should similarly select one initial problem where:

```text
need is substantial
+ current provision is inadequate
+ the mechanism is plausible
+ digital support is appropriate
+ outcomes are measurable
+ a service or institutional buyer exists
```

Our internal platform may be broad, but each market-facing proposition should remain specific.

## 1.2 Diagnose the pathway before choosing the product

A critical extension beyond the visible Big Health model is that Cognitive Support Labs should not assume in advance that an app is the correct solution.

We should first establish:

- where support is unavailable or delayed;
- which populations are missed;
- what users and practitioners need during the pathway;
- whether the primary problem is capacity, access, coordination, adaptation or intervention quality;
- where a digital component could add value without displacing necessary human care;
- whether a different service or workflow change would be more appropriate.

The first product may therefore be:

- a pathway map;
- a provision index;
- a needs assessment;
- an intervention-opportunity portfolio;
- a service-redesign recommendation;
- a low-intensity support protocol;
- a digital application;
- a practitioner tool;
- or a combined service model.

## 1.3 Build around an intervention, not a content library

Big Health products are structured programmes rather than collections of general wellbeing articles, videos and exercises.

A Cognitive Support Labs intervention should therefore have:

- a defined target population;
- a defined position in the pathway;
- a mechanism model;
- a programme duration;
- an adaptive or staged sequence;
- specified exercises and support components;
- outcome and safety boundaries;
- eligibility and exclusion rules;
- a completion definition;
- follow-up and maintenance logic.

The product should answer:

> What changes, through what mechanism, for whom, at which point in the pathway, over what period, and how will we know whether it mattered?

## 1.4 Treat evidence as part of the commercial infrastructure

Evidence is not simply something published after the product is complete.

It helps determine:

- whether practitioners will recommend it;
- whether universities or health services will adopt it;
- whether insurers or medical schemes will pay;
- whether users trust it;
- which claims can be made;
- whether the product can enter regulated or commissioned pathways;
- whether a service should continue, revise or discontinue it.

Cognitive Support Labs should build an evidence ladder for every intervention:

```text
pathway need and service-gap evidence
→ mechanism rationale
→ usability and acceptability
→ task-level change
→ protected transfer
→ independent-task transfer
→ functional outcome
→ service outcome
→ equity and access impact
→ economic value
→ implementation evidence
```

## 1.5 Separate the user from the customer

The person completing a programme may not be the party paying for it.

Potential Cognitive Support Labs customers include:

- universities;
- NHS and other public health services;
- private healthcare providers;
- medical schemes and insurers;
- employers and occupational-health providers;
- charities;
- digital-health platforms;
- public-health agencies;
- research and implementation partners;
- international health and education systems.

The commercial model can therefore be:

```text
institution identifies or commissions a support need
→ Cognitive Support Labs maps the pathway and develops the solution
→ individuals receive the intervention
→ practitioners or services receive appropriate, governed information
→ outcomes inform continuation, adaptation or scale
```

This is a B2B2C model.

## 1.6 Make implementation part of the product

A scientifically sound app can still fail if nobody knows:

- who should offer it;
- when it should be offered;
- who is unsuitable;
- how users are onboarded;
- what practitioners can see;
- how risk is escalated;
- what happens if engagement stops;
- how the product fits current care or support;
- how it will be funded;
- how accessibility and digital exclusion will be managed.

Cognitive Support Labs should therefore develop not only user applications but also:

- practitioner tools;
- pathway maps;
- onboarding routes;
- eligibility and exclusion rules;
- escalation procedures;
- progress summaries;
- implementation manuals;
- workforce and decision-rights specifications;
- service reports;
- APIs and integration options;
- accessibility and localisation resources.

## 1.7 Expand through a shared platform

Once one intervention has established:

- trust;
- infrastructure;
- measurement;
- distribution;
- governance;
- an implementation model;
- a buyer relationship;

the same underlying system can support adjacent interventions.

This is where the HRP Transfer Lab, shared measurement platform and modular app architecture become strategically important.

## Capability 1: Service-pathway, system and risk intelligence

### Core question

> **Where is psychological or functional support unavailable, delayed, fragmented, inconsistent, inequitable, unsafe or poorly matched to need?**

This should be the formal entry point into the Cognitive Support Labs model.

The aim is to understand the institutional system before selecting an intervention.

### 3.1 Cognitive Support Pathway Intelligence

Cognitive Support Labs could develop a standard method for analysing pathways across health, education, workplace, insurance and public-service systems.

For each system, the method would assess:

| Dimension | Core question |
|---|---|
| **Need** | Which psychological, cognitive-affective and functional difficulties are present? |
| **Provision** | What formal and informal support is currently available? |
| **Access** | Who receives support, through which route, at what threshold and at what time? |
| **Capacity** | Where do waits, staff shortages, hand-offs or eligibility rules restrict provision? |
| **Fit** | Does support adapt to heterogeneous needs or assume one standard user? |
| **Continuity** | What happens before, during and after formal intervention? |
| **Equity** | Which geographic, socioeconomic, cultural or demographic inequalities affect access? |
| **Risk** | Where can delay, fragmentation, inappropriate automation or poor escalation cause harm? |
| **Digital readiness** | Which parts of the pathway can safely and usefully support digital delivery? |
| **Outcome gap** | Where do users remain unsupported or continue to function poorly? |

The pathway should be mapped from the user’s perspective:

```text
difficulty emerges
→ information seeking
→ initial contact
→ triage or eligibility
→ waiting
→ assessment
→ intervention
→ follow-up
→ continuing functional support
```

At every stage, we would ask:

```text
Who is missed?
Who waits?
Who is excluded?
What needs remain unsupported?
Where is provision too generic?
Where is professional capacity consumed by avoidable work?
Where can low-intensity support complement rather than replace care?
Where are escalation and human judgement essential?
```

### 3.2 Methods

Pathway-intelligence projects could combine:

- public document and website analysis;
- structured provision audits;
- administrative and routine service data;
- referral and waiting-time analysis;
- service-capacity modelling;
- process mining where suitable data exist;
- geospatial and inequality analysis;
- provision and support indices;
- stakeholder interviews;
- user and lived-experience interviews;
- practitioner workshops;
- service blueprinting;
- risk and scenario modelling;
- comparative institutional case studies;
- human–AI role and decision-rights mapping where AI is part of the pathway.

AI research agents could assist with:

- searching and synthesising large document sets;
- coding pathway information;
- identifying inconsistencies;
- comparing institutional systems;
- generating candidate process maps;
- detecting possible gaps and data requirements.

Human experts would remain responsible for:

- interpreting service meaning;
- evaluating risk;
- validating findings with stakeholders;
- making consequential recommendations;
- determining whether an intervention should be developed.

### 3.3 Outputs

Capability 1 could produce:

- pathway and service maps;
- provision-gap analyses;
- waiting-pathway analyses;
- needs and inequality maps;
- service-capacity models;
- support-provision indices;
- risk registers;
- user-journey and service blueprints;
- digital-readiness assessments;
- ranked candidate insertion points;
- international market-entry intelligence;
- human–AI role maps and control-surface blueprints.

## Capability 3: Transfer-protocol engineering

### Core question

> **Which cognitive-affective process can be changed, and does the change generalise to meaningful functioning?**

This is the central role of the HRP Transfer Lab.

The operating sequence is:

```text
candidate mechanism
→ formal cognitive and behavioural model
→ competing intervention designs
→ synthetic experiments
→ prototype protocol
→ small dense human calibration
→ wrapper, context and functional-transfer tests
→ delayed re-check
→ protocol revision
```

The scientific distinction is:

> **Do not reward local task improvement alone. Test whether the useful operation survives changed materials, contexts, functional demands and delay.**

Capability 3 would generate:

- cognitive-affective intervention protocols;
- adaptive controllers;
- dosage and sequencing rules;
- wrapper and context-transfer ladders;
- active-control designs;
- functional missions;
- delayed re-checks;
- protocol manuals;
- versioned intervention specifications.

- sensitivity-to-change analysis;
- scoring-model comparison;

# 5. The HRP Transfer Lab as our shared innovation engine

## 5.1 What the Transfer Lab would do

The HRP Transfer Lab would be the research and development engine beneath Cognitive Support Labs.

Its purpose would be to reduce the time, cost and uncertainty involved in moving from a prioritised psychological or service opportunity to a testable intervention.

The operating sequence would be:

```text
pathway and provision evidence
→ prioritised intervention opportunity
→ candidate mechanism
→ formal intervention model
→ synthetic experiments
→ prototype protocol
→ small dense human calibration
→ custom digital application
→ controlled feasibility testing
→ functional and implementation validation
```

## 5.2 Synthetic participants

Synthetic participants would be computational representations of plausible user profiles and response patterns.

They could vary on dimensions such as:

- initial symptom and functional severity;
- attentional capture;
- interpretation bias;
- avoidance tendency;
- working-memory capacity;
- evidence sensitivity;
- learning rate;
- confidence calibration;
- engagement and dropout risk;
- intervention responsiveness;
- adherence;
- contextual instability;
- accessibility needs;
- service-pathway position.

We could simulate how different intervention designs might behave across these profiles.

For example:

```text
Protocol A
works well for high interpretation bias
but poorly for high avoidance

Protocol B
has weaker immediate task effects
but better predicted functional transfer

Protocol C
produces rapid gains
but may be too demanding for low-capacity users

Protocol D
requires fewer sessions
but may have a greater risk of relapse after delay
```

Synthetic experiments could help compare:

- dosage;
- task difficulty;
- session order;
- adaptive rules;
- intervention components;
- response thresholds;
- measurement schedules;
- likely ceiling and floor effects;
- dropout scenarios;
- trial designs;
- sample-size assumptions;
- active-control choices;
- subgroup adaptation;
- implementation scenarios.

## 5.3 What synthetic data cannot do

Synthetic participants would not demonstrate that an intervention works.

They would be used to:

```text
formalise assumptions
→ expose contradictions
→ reject weak designs
→ compare candidate protocols
→ improve measurement
→ prioritise prospective human testing
```

Human experimentation would remain necessary for:

- clinical or functional effects;
- acceptability;
- safety;
- lived experience;
- unexpected responses;
- adherence;
- transfer;
- implementation;
- equity;
- health-economic value.

The principle is:

> **Use synthetic experiments to fail cheaply and improve the design; use prospective human studies to determine whether effects are genuine.**

## 5.4 A learning system across products

As real data accumulate, the Transfer Lab could compare observed and simulated responses:

```text
synthetic prediction
→ human outcome
→ model discrepancy
→ revised participant and mechanism model
→ improved next protocol
```

Each project would improve:

- adherence models;
- psychometric models;
- common cognitive tasks;
- accessibility knowledge;
- intervention-response models;
- software components;
- implementation playbooks;
- control conditions;
- synthetic participant families;
- international localisation models.

The long-term moat would therefore not be one app.

It would be:

```text
pathway and provision datasets
```

# 8. The distinctive Cognitive Support Labs methodology

The central scientific proposition is:

> **We do not only ask whether users improve on the intervention task. We test whether the relevant cognitive-affective change transfers to new tasks, changed contexts, meaningful functioning and delayed use.**

The protocol sequence would be:

```text
identify the pathway need
→ train the target process
→ change the surface or scenario
→ measure the transfer cost
→ train recovery
→ introduce near-miss alternatives
→ test an independent task
→ deploy in a meaningful situation
→ re-check after delay
→ evaluate service and implementation value
```

For a health-psychology intervention, this might mean:

```text
interpretation improves on trained scenarios
→ test new scenarios
→ test a separate interpretation measure
→ test decisions under uncertainty
→ test activity or study behaviour
→ test later without prompting
→ examine effects on the service pathway
```

## 8.1 Evidence levels

| Evidence level | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Pathway need** | A meaningful support or service gap exists |
| **Engagement** | Users entered and completed the programme |
| **Local learning** | Performance improved on the trained operation |
| **Protected transfer** | Improvement survived new stimuli or parameters |
| **Independent transfer** | Improvement appeared on a separate task |
| **Functional transfer** | Relevant behaviour or functioning changed |
| **Service value** | Access, capacity, continuity or delivery improved |
| **Equity value** | Previously underserved groups gained meaningful access |
| **Delayed recovery** | Gains remained available after time had passed |
| **Implementation value** | The product could be used reliably in the pathway |
| **Economic value** | Benefits justified the implementation and delivery cost |

This is a stronger basis for scientific publication and commercial differentiation than relying on engagement or average symptom change alone.
